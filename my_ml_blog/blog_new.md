# My Journey Into Convolutional Neural Networks

## More Than Learning an Algorithm, Learning How to Learn

> "Every expert was once a beginner who refused to stop asking questions."

There is something almost magical about watching a computer recognize a face, classify a cat, or read handwritten digits with surprising accuracy. At first, it looks effortless. But once I stepped into the world of Artificial Intelligence, I began to see what was really behind the curtain: mathematics, experimentation, debugging, patience, and persistence.

This story is not only about Convolutional Neural Networks. It is about confusion, small victories, difficult lessons, and the slow realization that understanding does not arrive all at once. It grows layer by layer.

---

## Where It All Began

Artificial Intelligence had always sounded exciting to me.

Every day I saw examples of machines generating images, translating languages, helping doctors, and powering tools that seemed intelligent in ways I had only imagined before. Naturally, I became curious.

How could a computer learn something humans spend years mastering?

The more I read, the more questions I had. Eventually, I realized that if I truly wanted to understand AI, I could not remain a spectator. I had to learn how it worked from the inside.

That decision marked the beginning of one of the most challenging and rewarding learning experiences of my life.

At first, I thought learning AI would mainly involve coding.

I was wrong.

Artificial Intelligence is mathematics.

It is statistics.

It is logic.

It is problem solving.

Most of all, it is a new way of thinking.

---

## My First Reality Check

Like many beginners, I wanted to jump straight into the exciting parts.

I wanted to build image classifiers.

I wanted to detect objects.

I wanted to create intelligent systems.

Instead, I found myself staring at linear algebra, probability, gradients, derivatives, optimization, and neural networks.

That was humbling.

I kept asking myself:

> Why do I need all this mathematics before I can build something interesting?

At that stage, math felt like a barrier instead of a tool. I saw symbols, not meaning. I opened tutorials, watched a few minutes, became overwhelmed, and closed everything again.

The problem was not lack of interest. The problem was trying to understand everything at once.

That was my first lesson.

Learning Artificial Intelligence is not a sprint.

It is a marathon.

---

## Learning to Slow Down

One evening, after hours of trying to make concepts fit together, I stopped chasing speed. I changed the question.

Instead of asking:

> How quickly can I finish this course?

I started asking:

> What is the one thing I can truly understand today?

That shift changed everything.

Instead of rushing through ten topics, I focused on one. Instead of collecting lectures, I paused and asked myself whether I could explain the idea in my own words. If I could not, I stayed with it a little longer.

I stopped measuring progress by how much content I finished.

I started measuring progress by how much I actually understood.

Ironically, slowing down made me learn faster.

---

## Confusion Became Part of the Process

One thing no tutorial prepared me for was how often I would feel confused.

At first, confusion felt like failure. Whenever I could not understand something after one reading, I assumed I was not smart enough.

Later, I learned the truth: confusion is often a sign that the brain is building something new.

I started keeping a notebook, not to copy definitions, but to write questions.

* Why do we need activation functions?
* Why can one layer not solve everything?
* What exactly is a weight?
* How does a computer know whether it made the right prediction?
* Why does changing one number improve an entire model?

At first, those pages made me feel inexperienced.

Later, they became proof of growth.

The notebook became less of a record of what I knew and more of a record of how I learned.

---

## The Mathematics I Did Not Know I Needed

One of the biggest surprises in my journey was discovering that mathematics was not my enemy. It was my guide.

I will always remember Mr. Buri Gershom with gratitude. Long before I seriously studied Artificial Intelligence, he taught me Discrete Mathematics and Multivariable Calculus. At the time, like many students, I wondered when those lessons would ever matter.

Why spend so much time on functions, gradients, limits, matrices, and logical reasoning?

The answer came much later.

As I moved deeper into deep learning, I began seeing familiar ideas hiding inside unfamiliar terms.

Optimization was no longer just a chapter from calculus. It became the process through which a neural network learns.

Partial derivatives were no longer classroom exercises. They became the language of backpropagation.

Logical reasoning from Discrete Mathematics helped me think systematically about algorithms, conditions, and computation.

Suddenly, subjects that once felt separate were connected.

I realized that education is rarely wasted.

Sometimes knowledge only waits for the right moment to reveal its value.

That realization changed how I view learning forever.

No topic exists in isolation.

Everything connects.

Sometimes we only need enough experience to notice it.

---

## Learning Neural Networks Before CNNs

Eventually I reached neural networks.

I thought this would be the exciting part.

Instead, it felt like standing at the base of another mountain.

Neurons.

Weights.

Biases.

Activation functions.

Forward propagation.

Loss functions.

Backpropagation.

Every chapter introduced another unfamiliar word.

Every explanation seemed to assume I already understood the previous one.

I remember staring at diagrams full of circles and arrows while everyone called them "simple." To me, they looked anything but simple.

There were moments when I genuinely wondered whether I had chosen a field that was beyond my ability.

So I changed my approach.

Rather than trying to master the mathematics immediately, I focused on the story.

What problem is this network solving?

How does information move?

Why does each layer exist?

Why can we not skip this step?

Once I understood the purpose, the equations began to make sense.

They stopped looking like random symbols and started looking like explanations.

That was another important lesson.

Understanding often begins with curiosity, not calculation.

---

## The Habit That Changed Everything

If there is one habit that transformed my learning, it was learning to teach myself.

After every study session, I would close my laptop and ask:

> Can I explain this without looking at my notes?

If the answer was no, I knew I had not really understood it yet.

So I would go back.

Read again.

Draw diagrams.

Watch another explanation.

Sometimes I searched for three or four different teachers explaining the same topic. One analogy might fail, and another would suddenly click.

That taught me something valuable:

Struggling with a topic does not always mean you need more effort. Sometimes you just need a different explanation.

That realization gave me patience.

Learning stopped feeling like a test of intelligence and started feeling like a search for understanding.

---

## Meeting Convolutional Neural Networks

After weeks of learning the foundations, I finally reached Convolutional Neural Networks.

I thought I had already climbed the hardest part.

I was wrong.

Convolution.

Filters.

Kernels.

Feature maps.

Padding.

Stride.

Pooling.

Flattening.

Each term added another layer of complexity, and the mountain suddenly looked even steeper.

For a brief moment, I laughed and thought:

> So... we are starting all over again?

---

## The Question That Changed Everything

One of the most important lessons in my journey was this: the right question matters more than memorizing the right answer.

When I first encountered CNNs, I stopped trying to understand every definition immediately. Instead, I asked one question:

> How does a computer actually see an image?

That question changed everything.

As humans, we do not consciously think in pixels. We see a dog and recognize it as a dog without calculating the position of its ears or the distance between its eyes. Our brains learn patterns naturally over time.

Computers do not see images that way.

To a computer, an image is a grid of numbers.

Thousands of numbers.

Sometimes millions.

That realization fascinated me.

Somehow, a model had to learn meaningful patterns from nothing but numerical values.

CNNs were not just another algorithm.

They were an attempt to teach computers how to look at the world.

---

## My First Encounter with Convolution

If I had to name the concept that challenged me the most early on, it would be convolution.

I opened a textbook and saw kernels sliding across matrices. Within minutes, I felt overwhelmed.

I read again.

Still confused.

I watched another video.

Slightly better.

Then another.

Still not enough.

It felt like people were explaining how convolution worked, but not why it worked.

That difference mattered to me.

I did not want to memorize a process. I wanted to understand the reason behind it.

So I stepped away from the mathematics for a while and looked for visual explanations.

Watching a filter move across an image was one of the biggest turning points in my learning. For the first time, I could see the idea instead of only reading about it.

The filter looked at a small region, extracted useful information, and moved on.

Piece by piece.

Section by section.

That visualization helped me more than hours of reading equations.

It taught me a lesson I still carry today:

> When something feels too abstract, make it visual.

---

## Small Victories Matter

One mistake I made early was expecting huge breakthroughs every day.

I wanted every study session to end with complete understanding.

Reality was different.

Some days I studied for hours and felt like I had learned almost nothing.

Other days, a five-minute explanation suddenly connected ideas that had confused me for weeks.

So I started celebrating smaller wins.

Understanding what a kernel does.

Learning why padding exists.

Realizing why stride changes the output size.

Those victories may seem small, but together they built the foundation for everything that came later.

Deep learning itself mirrors that process.

Just as a CNN gradually builds complex features from simple ones, I was gradually building understanding from simple ideas.

Learning was happening layer by layer.

And in a strange way, CNNs were also teaching me how humans learn.

---

## Pooling Taught Me That Less Can Be More

When I reached pooling layers, I thought I had finally found something simple.

Pooling reduces the size of feature maps while keeping the important information.

Easy enough.

Then I asked:

> If we remove information, are we making the model worse?

That question stayed with me for a while.

Eventually, I realized something useful.

Not every detail matters.

In machine learning and in life, focusing on what matters often gives better results than trying to keep everything.

Pooling is not about throwing information away randomly. It is about preserving what is important.

That idea changed the way I studied too.

Instead of memorizing every sentence in my notes, I started identifying the key ideas.

Understanding replaced memorization.

Depth replaced quantity.

---

## When Code Refused to Cooperate

Eventually theory had to become practice.

It was time to build my own CNN.

I was excited. I imagined writing a few lines of code, pressing Run, and watching the model learn magically.

Reality had different plans.

My first attempt failed.

Then the second.

Then the third.

Sometimes I forgot a bracket.

Sometimes the tensor shapes were wrong.

Sometimes images would not load.

Sometimes the model would train and still produce terrible predictions.

At first, that was frustrating.

Eventually, I realized the error messages were not the enemy.

They were clues.

Instead of panicking every time I saw red text in the terminal, I started reading carefully.

Sometimes the real problem was hidden in the last sentence.

Sometimes one small mistake earlier in the code caused a chain of confusing errors later.

Debugging became less frightening.

I stopped seeing bugs as proof that I was not good enough.

I started seeing them as puzzles.

That shift made programming far more enjoyable.

---

## Learning From the Ground Up

Around this time, I came across the work of Andrej Karpathy.

His way of teaching stood out immediately.

Rather than treating neural networks as mysterious tools hidden behind libraries, he encouraged learners to build them from scratch.

That changed my perspective.

Before that, using TensorFlow or PyTorch sometimes felt like driving a car without knowing what was happening under the hood. The car moved, but I could not explain why.

Karpathy's approach challenged that.

By building each component yourself, starting with simple neurons, implementing forward passes, calculating gradients, and understanding backpropagation, you build intuition that no high-level library can give you alone.

One idea stayed with me:

> Do not just use neural networks. Understand them.

That became my goal.

Whenever I met a new concept, I resisted the urge to accept it as magic. Instead, I asked myself:

> Could I explain this if TensorFlow and PyTorch were removed?

If the answer was no, I knew there was more to learn.

It slowed me down at first.

But in the long run, it made me much stronger.

---

## Learning to Be a Beginner

One of the hardest things was not convolution.

It was accepting that I was still a beginner.

It is easy to compare yourself with experienced researchers and engineers. You see someone explain a difficult concept with ease, and it is tempting to think:

> Maybe I am just not good enough.

I had moments like that too.

But over time, I realized something important.

Every expert I admired had once struggled with the same confusion, the same bugs, and the same questions.

The difference was not that they never struggled.

The difference was that they kept going.

That thought removed a lot of pressure.

I stopped expecting myself to understand everything immediately.

I gave myself permission to be a student.

To ask questions.

To revisit concepts.

To make mistakes.

To learn slowly.

The moment I stopped trying to look knowledgeable was the moment I started learning more deeply.

## Learning by Building

Reading about neural networks and building one are two completely different experiences.

It is like learning how to swim from a book. You can understand every technique, but until you step into the water, you do not truly know how to swim.

The same was true for deep learning.

At some point, I had to stop reading and start building.

That was where the real learning began.

I still remember the excitement of writing my first model.

Convolutional layers.

Pooling layers.

Flattening.

Fully connected layers.

Compilation.

Everything looked familiar from the examples I had studied.

I clicked Run.

It failed.

That cycle repeated more times than I expected.

But each failure taught me something new.

## Error Messages Became Teachers

At first, error messages felt personal.

Every red line seemed to say:

> You do not know what you are doing.

Later, I understood something better.

The computer was not insulting me. It was communicating.

It was telling me exactly where it had become confused.

That changed the way I debugged.

I started asking better questions.

What is this error actually saying?

What does the function expect?

What am I giving it instead?

Why are the dimensions different?

I also learned to respect small details.

A misplaced parenthesis.

A forgotten comma.

An incorrect image path.

A typo in a variable name.

Small mistakes can stop an entire model from working.

Programming teaches humility.

Computers do not reward assumptions.

They reward precision.

---

## The Importance of Understanding Before Searching

One habit I am especially grateful for is resisting the urge to search online immediately whenever I hit an error.

There is nothing wrong with documentation or asking for help. They helped me many times.

But before searching, I trained myself to investigate first.

I read the traceback carefully.

I checked the line that failed.

I compared it with earlier code.

I printed intermediate outputs.

I checked tensor shapes.

Sometimes I solved the problem myself.

Sometimes I did not.

But even when I eventually searched for the answer, I understood it much better because I had already spent time thinking.

That process strengthened my problem-solving skills far more than copying solutions ever could.

Learning happens during the struggle, not just after the answer appears.

---

## Data Matters More Than I Expected

Once my model finally started running, I thought the difficult part was over.

Again, I was wrong.

The model trained successfully, but the accuracy was not impressive. Sometimes it barely improved at all.

I immediately started changing the architecture.

More layers.

More filters.

Different activation functions.

Different optimizers.

Different learning rates.

Sometimes the results became worse.

That is when I realized I had been focusing too much on the model and not enough on the data.

Until then, I had treated datasets as something that simply existed.

Download them.

Load them.

Train the model.

Simple.

But real machine learning begins before training.

Images need resizing.

Pixel values often need normalization.

Training and testing sets must be split correctly.

Classes should be balanced whenever possible.

Sometimes images need cleaning or augmentation.

I learned that successful machine learning is not only about building intelligent models.

It is also about preparing meaningful data.

The model can only learn from what it receives.

If the data is poor, the understanding will be poor too.

That lesson changed how I think about AI.

Strong foundations matter.

---

## The Day I Met Overfitting

One of the most confusing moments in my journey came when I trained a model that achieved excellent training accuracy.

I was thrilled.

Then I tested it on unseen images.

The performance dropped dramatically.

I did not understand it at first.

How could a model do so well during training and still struggle with new data?

The answer was overfitting.

The model had not really learned general patterns. It had become too good at remembering the training examples.

That realization made me think about learning in general.

A student who memorizes answers without understanding concepts may perform well on familiar questions, but struggle with new ones.

A truly good learner understands the principle behind the answer.

Neural networks are not so different.

Whether human or artificial, real learning involves generalization.

---

## Experimentation Without Fear

Once I understood that there was no single perfect CNN architecture, I became more willing to experiment.

I changed the number of convolutional layers.

I adjusted filter sizes.

I modified learning rates.

I added dropout.

I introduced data augmentation.

I changed batch sizes.

Sometimes the model improved.

Sometimes it became worse.

Instead of treating failure as disappointment, I started asking:

> What did this experiment teach me?

That question changed my mindset.

Every unsuccessful attempt became useful information.

Every unexpected result helped me understand the model a little better.

Progress is rarely a straight line.

It is built from curiosity, observation, experimentation, and reflection.

The more you learn, the more you realize how much there is still to discover.

It excites me.
