# My Journey into Convolutional Neural Networks

### More Than Learning an Algorithm—Learning How to Learn

*"Every expert was once a beginner who refused to stop asking questions."*

There is something fascinating about watching a computer recognize a face, distinguish between a cat and a dog, or identify handwritten digits with remarkable accuracy. At first glance, it almost feels magical. Like many people stepping into the world of Artificial Intelligence, I was amazed by what machines could do. But as I would soon discover, behind every impressive AI application lies thousands of hours of research, mathematics, programming, experimentation, failure, and persistence.

This is not just a story about learning Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs). It is a story about learning patience, embracing confusion, appreciating mathematics in a completely different way, and realizing that understanding comes one small step at a time.

Looking back, I can honestly say that my journey wasn't defined by the moments when everything worked perfectly. Instead, it was shaped by the moments when nothing made sense, when my code refused to run, when concepts seemed impossible to understand, and when I wondered whether I had chosen a path that was simply too difficult for me.

Yet every obstacle taught me something valuable.

This is my journey.

---

# Where It All Began

Artificial Intelligence had always sounded exciting to me.

Every day I came across news about machines generating images, translating languages, driving cars, and helping doctors diagnose diseases. Naturally, I became curious.

How could a computer possibly learn something that humans spend years mastering?

The more I read, the more questions I had.

Eventually I realized that if I truly wanted to understand AI, I couldn't remain someone who simply admired the technology from a distance. I needed to understand how it worked.

That decision marked the beginning of one of the most challenging—and rewarding—learning experiences I have ever had.

At the beginning, I imagined learning AI would simply involve writing code.

I quickly discovered how wrong I was.

Artificial Intelligence is not just programming.

It is mathematics.

It is statistics.

It is logic.

It is problem solving.

Most importantly, it is learning how to think differently.

---

# My First Reality Check

Like many beginners, I was eager to jump straight into exciting topics.

I wanted to build image classifiers.

I wanted to recognize objects.

I wanted to create intelligent systems.

Instead, I found myself reading about linear algebra, probability, gradients, derivatives, optimization, and neural networks.

To be honest, I felt disappointed.

I kept asking myself,

*"Why do I have to learn all this mathematics before I can build something interesting?"*

At that point, mathematics felt like an obstacle rather than a tool.

I didn't yet understand that every equation represented an idea.

I only saw complicated symbols.

For several days, I found myself opening tutorials, watching videos for a few minutes, becoming overwhelmed, and closing everything again.

It wasn't because I lacked interest.

It was because I was trying to understand everything at once.

That was my first lesson.

Learning Artificial Intelligence is not a sprint.

It is a marathon.

---

# Learning to Slow Down

One evening, after spending hours trying to understand concepts that refused to make sense, I decided to stop chasing speed.

Instead of asking,

*"How quickly can I finish this course?"*

I started asking,

*"What is the one thing I can truly understand today?"*

That small change transformed my learning.

Instead of rushing through ten topics, I focused on one.

Instead of watching hours of lectures without reflection, I paused frequently and asked myself,

*"Can I explain this in my own words?"*

If the answer was no, I wasn't ready to move on.

This became one of the most important habits throughout my journey.

I stopped measuring progress by the number of videos I completed.

Instead, I measured progress by the number of ideas I genuinely understood.

Oddly enough, slowing down helped me learn much faster.

---

# Discovering That Confusion Is Part of Learning

One thing no tutorial had prepared me for was how often I would feel confused.

At first, confusion felt like failure.

Whenever I couldn't understand something after reading it once, I assumed I wasn't smart enough.

Looking back now, I smile at that thought.

The truth is that confusion is often a sign that your brain is building new connections.

Every difficult concept stretches your thinking.

Every question forces you to reorganize what you already know.

I began keeping a notebook—not to copy definitions—but to write questions.

Sometimes an entire page contained nothing but questions.

* Why do we need activation functions?
* Why can't one layer solve everything?
* What exactly is a weight?
* How does a computer know whether it made the right prediction?
* Why does changing one number improve an entire model?

At first those pages made me feel ignorant.

Months later, they became proof of my growth.

Most of those questions eventually found answers.

The notebook became less of a record of what I knew and more of a record of how I learned.

# The Mathematics I Didn't Know I Needed

One of the biggest surprises in my journey was discovering that mathematics wasn't my enemy.

It was my guide.

There is one lecturer I will always remember with gratitude: Buri Gershom.

Long before I seriously studied Artificial Intelligence, he taught me Discrete Mathematics and Multivariable Calculus.

At the time, like many students, I often wondered when I would ever use some of those concepts in the real world.

Why spend so much time learning functions, gradients, limits, optimization, matrices, and logical reasoning?

The answers came much later.

As I progressed into deep learning, I began recognizing familiar ideas hidden beneath unfamiliar terminology.

Optimization was no longer just a topic from calculus.

It became the process through which a neural network learns.

Partial derivatives were no longer classroom exercises.

They became the language of backpropagation.

Logical reasoning from Discrete Mathematics helped me think systematically about algorithms, conditions, and computational problems.

Suddenly, subjects that once seemed separate became deeply connected.

One of the most rewarding moments in my learning journey was realizing that education is rarely wasted.

Sometimes knowledge simply waits for the right moment to reveal its value.

I found myself appreciating those mathematics classes in a completely new way.

Not because they made CNNs easy.

But because they gave me the mindset needed to tackle difficult problems patiently and logically.

That realization changed how I viewed learning forever.

No topic exists in isolation.

Everything connects.

Sometimes we simply need enough experience to see the connections.

# The Habit That Changed Everything

If there is one habit that transformed my learning more than anything else, it was learning to teach myself.

After every study session, I would close my laptop and pretend someone had asked me,

*"Can you explain this without looking at your notes?"*

If I couldn't explain it simply, I knew I hadn't really understood it.

So I would return to the material.

Read again.

Draw diagrams.

Watch another explanation.

Sometimes I searched for three or four different teachers explaining the same idea.

Each person explained concepts differently.

One analogy would fail.

Another would suddenly make perfect sense.

I learned that struggling with a topic doesn't always mean you need more effort.

Sometimes you simply need a different explanation.

That realization gave me confidence.

Instead of blaming myself whenever I became confused, I became more patient.

But for the first time, I truly believed I could climb it.

I had finally reached a point where terms like *neurons*, *weights*, *biases*, and *activation functions* no longer sounded completely foreign. I wasn't an expert, but I was beginning to understand the language of deep learning.

Naturally, I thought the hardest part was behind me.

I was wrong.

The moment I opened my first lesson on Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs), it felt as though I had stepped into an entirely new world.

Convolution.

Filters.

Kernels.

Feature maps.

Padding.

Stride.

Pooling.

Flattening.

Each new term seemed to bring another layer of complexity. It reminded me of my first encounter with neural networks, except this time the mountain looked even taller.

# Asking the Question That Changed Everything

One thing I have learned throughout my journey is that asking the right question is often more valuable than memorizing the right answer.

When I first encountered CNNs, I stopped trying to understand every definition immediately.

Instead, I asked one simple question.

**How does a computer actually see an image?**

It sounds like a basic question, but it completely changed how I approached learning.

As humans, we don't consciously think about pixels. When we see a dog, we simply recognize it as a dog. We don't measure the distance between its eyes or calculate the angle of its ears. Our brains have learned those patterns naturally over many years.

Computers, however, don't see images the way we do.

To a computer, an image is simply a grid of numbers.

Thousands—or even millions—of numbers.

That realization fascinated me.

Somehow, a model had to learn meaningful patterns from nothing more than numerical values.

Suddenly, CNNs weren't just another algorithm.

They were an attempt to teach computers how to "look" at the world.

# My First Encounter with Convolution

If I had to choose the concept that challenged me the most in the early stages, it would undoubtedly be convolution.

I remember opening a textbook and immediately seeing mathematical notation describing kernels sliding across matrices.

Within minutes, I felt overwhelmed.

I tried reading the explanation again.

Still confused.

I watched another video.

Slightly better.

Then another.

Still not enough.

It felt as though everyone was explaining *how* convolution worked but not *why* it worked.

That difference mattered to me.

I didn't want to memorize a process.

I wanted to understand the reason behind it.

So I stepped away from the mathematics for a while.

Instead, I searched for visual demonstrations.

Watching an animation of a small filter moving across an image was one of the biggest turning points in my learning journey.

For the first time, I could actually see what was happening.

Instead of analyzing the entire image at once, the filter looked at one small region, extracted useful information, then moved to the next region.

Piece by piece.

Section by section.

Almost like reading a page one line at a time instead of trying to read every word simultaneously.

That single visualization helped me more than hours of reading equations.

It taught me an important lesson that I still carry today:

**When something feels too abstract, find a way to make it visual.**

# Learning to Appreciate Small Victories

One mistake I made early in my learning journey was expecting huge breakthroughs every day.

I wanted every study session to end with complete understanding.

Reality was very different.

Some days I studied for two hours and felt like I had learned almost nothing.

Other days, a five-minute explanation suddenly connected ideas that had confused me for weeks.

I slowly began celebrating small victories.

Understanding what a kernel does.

Learning why padding exists.

Realizing why stride changes the output size.

I learned that deep learning itself mirrors the learning process.

Just as a CNN gradually builds complex features from simple ones, I was gradually building complex understanding from simple ideas.

Learning wasn't happening all at once.

It was happening layer by layer.

Ironically, CNNs were teaching me how humans learn too.

# Pooling: Learning That Less Can Be More

When I reached pooling layers, I initially thought,

*"Finally, something simpler."*

The explanation seemed straightforward.

Pooling reduces the size of feature maps while keeping the important information.

Easy enough.

Or so I thought.

Then another question appeared.

*"If we're removing information, aren't we making the model worse?"*

That question stayed with me for quite some time.

Eventually, I realized something beautiful.

Not every detail matters.

Whether in machine learning or in life, focusing on what is important often produces better results than trying to remember everything.

Pooling isn't about throwing information away randomly.

It is about preserving what matters most.

That simple idea extended beyond CNNs.

It changed the way I studied.

Instead of trying to memorize every sentence in my notes, I started identifying the key ideas.

Understanding replaced memorization.

Depth replaced quantity.

They were trying to teach me.

I stopped seeing bugs as evidence that I wasn't good enough.

Instead, I started seeing them as clues in a puzzle waiting to be solved.

# Discovering the Power of Building from Scratch

Around this stage of my journey, I came across the work of Andrej Karpathy.

His approach immediately stood out to me.

Rather than treating neural networks as mysterious tools hidden behind software libraries, he encouraged learners to build them from the ground up.

Watching him implement neural networks from scratch changed the way I thought about learning.

Before then, using frameworks like TensorFlow or PyTorch almost felt like driving a car without knowing what was happening under the hood.

Yes, the car moved.

Yes, it reached its destination.

But I couldn't explain why it worked.

Karpathy's philosophy challenged that way of thinking.

He demonstrated that by building each component yourself—starting with simple neurons, implementing forward passes, calculating gradients, and understanding backpropagation—you develop an intuition that no high-level library can give you on its own.

One statement, although simple, stayed with me throughout my journey:

**Don't just use neural networks. Understand them.**

That became my new goal.

Whenever I encountered a new concept, I resisted the temptation to accept it as "magic."

Instead, I asked,

*"Could I explain this if someone removed TensorFlow and PyTorch?"*

If the answer was no, I knew there was still more to learn.

This approach slowed my progress in the beginning.

Ironically, it accelerated my understanding in the long run.

# Learning to Read Before Searching

One habit I'm particularly grateful for developing was resisting the temptation to immediately search online whenever I encountered an error.

However, before opening a search engine, I challenged myself to spend a few minutes investigating the problem on my own.

Compare it with previous code.

Print intermediate outputs.

Check tensor shapes.

That process strengthened my problem-solving skills far more than copying solutions ever could.

# Discovering That Data Matters More Than I Expected

Once my model finally started running, I assumed the difficult part was over.

Again, I was wrong.

The model trained successfully.

The accuracy wasn't impressive.

Sometimes it barely improved at all.

I immediately started changing the architecture.

More layers.

More filters.

Different activation functions.

Different optimizers.

Different learning rates.

Sometimes the results became even worse.

It took me a while to realize that I had been focusing on the model while overlooking something equally important.

The data.

Until then, I had treated datasets as something that simply existed.

Download them.

Load them.

Train the model.

Simple.

As I learned more, I began appreciating how much work happens before training even begins.

Images need to be resized.

Pixel values often need normalization.

Training and testing sets must be separated correctly.

Classes should be balanced whenever possible.

Sometimes images need cleaning or augmentation.

I discovered that successful machine learning is not only about building intelligent models.

It is also about preparing meaningful data.

That lesson completely changed how I viewed AI.

The model can only learn from the information it receives.

If the data is poor, the model's understanding will also be poor.

It was another reminder that strong foundations matter.

# The Day I Met Overfitting

One of the most confusing moments in my journey happened when I trained a model that achieved excellent training accuracy.

I was thrilled.

Finally!

Everything seemed to be working.

Then I tested the model on unseen images.

The performance dropped dramatically.

I couldn't understand it.

How could a model perform so well during training but struggle so much with new data?

Had I done something wrong?

After reading more about the problem, I encountered a word that every machine learning practitioner eventually meets.

Overfitting.

The model hadn't truly learned to recognize general patterns.

Instead, it had become extremely good at remembering the training examples.

That realization fascinated me.

In many ways, it reminded me of education.

A student who memorizes answers without understanding concepts may perform well on familiar questions but struggle with unfamiliar ones.

A truly good learner understands the underlying principles.

Neural networks are surprisingly similar.

That insight changed the way I thought about learning itself.

Whether human or artificial, real learning involves generalization.

# Advice to Anyone Beginning This Journey

If someone reading this is just beginning to learn Convolutional Neural Networks, I would like to share a few lessons that I wish someone had shared with me.

First, do not rush.

Artificial Intelligence is not a race.

Understanding one concept deeply is worth far more than quickly finishing ten tutorials.

Second, do not fear mathematics.

You do not have to master every equation on your first day.

Begin by understanding the intuition.

Ask why an idea exists before worrying about how every formula is derived.

As your understanding grows, the mathematics becomes much more meaningful.

Third, build things.

Read books.

Watch lectures.

But eventually, write the code yourself.

Break it.

Fix it.

Experiment with it.

There is no substitute for learning through experience.

Fourth, ask questions without embarrassment.

Some of the questions that felt "too simple" to ask eventually became the questions that unlocked my understanding.

Curiosity is never a weakness.

Finally, be patient with yourself.

You are not competing against researchers with decades of experience.

You are simply trying to become a little better than you were yesterday.

That is enough.
