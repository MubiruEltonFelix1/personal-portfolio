Overfitting vs Underfitting — The Bias-Variance Tradeoff

7 min read Intermediate

You've trained your model. On the training data, accuracy is 99.7%. You're feeling great. Then you test it on new data — and accuracy drops to 72%. What happened? Overfitting. Your model memorized the training examples instead of learning the underlying pattern.

This is the central tension in machine learning. Let's understand it properly.

The Three States of a Model

Underfitting (High Bias)

The model is too simple to capture the pattern in the data. It performs poorly on both training and test data. Think of trying to fit a straight line through points that form a parabola — no matter how you angle the line, it can't curve.

Symptoms: High training error, high test error. Loss barely improves during training.

Just Right (Good Generalization)

The model captures the true underlying pattern without being distracted by noise. Training error is low and test error is similarly low. This is the sweet spot.

Symptoms: Training and validation loss both decreasing together, small gap between them.

Overfitting (High Variance)

The model is so complex it memorizes individual training examples — including their noise and quirks. It performs brilliantly on training data but fails on anything new. Think of a student who memorized the answer key instead of understanding the concepts.

Symptoms: Training loss continues decreasing while validation loss starts increasing. That divergence is the classic overfitting signature.

"An overfit model knows the training set by heart. A well-trained model understands the subject."

The Bias-Variance Tradeoff

This is the formal name for the underfitting-overfitting spectrum:

  • Bias — error from overly simplistic assumptions. High bias = underfitting.
  • Variance — error from excessive sensitivity to small fluctuations in the training data. High variance = overfitting.

You can't minimize both simultaneously with a fixed model capacity. Increasing model complexity reduces bias but increases variance. The art is finding the sweet spot — and modern deep learning has some surprising tricks.

How to Detect Overfitting

Train/Validation Split

The single most important practice. Keep a portion of your data (typically 15-20%) completely separate during training. Monitor loss on both sets. If training loss drops while validation loss rises — you're overfitting.

Learning Curves

Plot loss vs. epoch for both training and validation sets. A widening gap between the two curves is the clearest overfitting signal. If both curves are high and flat, you're underfitting.

Fixes for Overfitting

1. More Data

The most effective fix. Overfitting happens when the model has enough capacity to memorize the training set. More data makes memorization harder and forces the model to learn general patterns. If you can't collect more real data, use data augmentation (rotations, flips, noise for images; synonym replacement for text).

2. Regularization

L1/L2 regularization adds a penalty for large weights to the loss function. Large weights often indicate the model is over-relying on specific features. L2 (weight decay) is the standard — it encourages small, distributed weights rather than a few large ones.

Dropout randomly deactivates a fraction of neurons during each training step. This forces the network to build redundancy — no single neuron can become indispensable. At test time, all neurons are active. Dropout rates of 0.2-0.5 are common.

3. Early Stopping

Monitor validation loss during training. When it stops improving for N consecutive epochs (patience), stop training. This prevents the model from entering the overfitting regime. Simple and surprisingly effective.

4. Reduce Model Complexity

Fewer layers, fewer neurons, or simpler architectures. A model with millions of parameters trained on thousands of examples is a recipe for memorization. The rule of thumb: your dataset should be at least 10x larger than your number of parameters for training from scratch.

Fixes for Underfitting

  • Train longer — the model may simply need more epochs
  • Increase model capacity — more layers, more neurons
  • Reduce regularization — you might be constraining the model too much
  • Better features — the input data may not contain enough signal
  • Lower learning rate — the optimizer might be overshooting the minimum

The Double Descent Surprise

Modern deep learning has revealed a twist: in very large models, after the point where the model is big enough to perfectly fit the training data, test error can decrease again as you make the model even larger. This "double descent" phenomenon challenges the classical bias-variance picture and is an active area of research. Large language models like GPT-4 exhibit this: they're vastly over-parameterized relative to their training data, yet generalize remarkably well.

The bias-variance tradeoff is your mental model for debugging model performance. Train/validation split, learning curves, and the fixes above will cover 95% of real-world cases. The remaining 5% is where research happens.
